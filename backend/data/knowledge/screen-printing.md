## What the process does

Ink is pushed through a mesh stencil, one screen per colour. The screen is the
tooling: it costs money and time to make, then costs almost nothing to use.

## Cost shape

Setup dominates small orders. A three-colour design needs three screens, and that
cost is the same whether the run is 50 pieces or 5000. This is why screen printing
is usually the cheapest option at volume and among the most expensive per unit
below roughly 100 pieces.

Ask any quoting partner to separate **setup and screen cost** from **per-unit
cost**. A single price-each figure hides which of the two is driving the total.

## Where it is strong

- Flat or gently curved surfaces, textiles above all.
- Solid, saturated spot colours, including specified Pantone matches.
- Durability on flexible substrates. With the right ink system it survives flexing
  and washing better than most transfers.
- Opaque coverage on dark goods, because the ink layer is thick.

## Where it struggles

- Photographic images and fine gradients.
- Designs with many colours. Each one adds a screen, a setup and a registration
  risk.
- Fine detail below roughly 1 mm line weight, which clogs the mesh.

## Ink must match the substrate

Ink choice is not a detail. Plastisol adheres well to vinyl and PVC surfaces,
while water-based inks are softer on cotton but may not bond to synthetics at
all. Adhesion on rubber and TPE usually needs a primer or a bonding additive,
which is exactly the kind of thing a physical sample confirms and a quote does
not.
