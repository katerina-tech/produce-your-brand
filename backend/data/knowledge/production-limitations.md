## Lead time is not just production time

A quoted "10 working days" usually means days in production. The real timeline is:

1. Artwork review and correction, one to three days, longer if files need fixing.
2. Sample production and approval, three to seven days plus shipping each way.
3. Production, as quoted.
4. Shipping and customs, one to two days domestically, longer cross-border.

Working backwards from a fixed date, plan against the total. A five-day buffer for
artwork approval and delivery is a realistic minimum, and it is the reason a
partner quoting a lead time close to the available window should be treated as
tight rather than feasible.

## Minimum order quantities are about setup, not capability

An MOQ usually exists to amortise a fixed cost: a screen, a die, a digitising
fee, a machine changeover. A partner quoting a 500-piece minimum on a 100-piece
order is not refusing the work, but the setup cost lands on 100 units instead of
500. Sometimes it is worth paying; sometimes another partner is simply set up for
short runs.

## Always ask for a sample when

- The substrate is heat-sensitive, textured, or of unconfirmed composition.
- The finish is metallic or otherwise dependent on a physical effect.
- Colour matching matters commercially.
- The customer supplies the goods, because a failed test destroys their stock,
  not the partner stock.

## Customer-supplied goods

Working on goods the customer already owns changes the risk. Scrap is the
customer loss, and most partners will not accept liability for it. Confirm in
writing: acceptance of external goods, expected spoilage allowance, whether they
require spare units for setup, inbound delivery arrangements, and what happens to
rejects. A spoilage allowance of a few percent is normal and should be agreed
before production rather than discovered after it.
